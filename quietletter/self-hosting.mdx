The Quietletter server is also available as an **open-source core** (`quietletter-core`, licensed
**AGPL-3.0**) that you can run yourself. The app connects to it the same way it connects to the hosted
service, so what you set up doesn't depend on any one company staying alive.

## What you need

- **Docker** with Docker Compose — or Node.js 24.16+ and your own PostgreSQL database (see
    [Without Docker](/quietletter/running-your-instance#without-docker)).
- **A domain with HTTPS.** The core serves plain HTTP; put it behind a reverse proxy that provides
    TLS (Caddy, nginx, Traefik…). The app only connects over **HTTPS**, except to addresses on your
    local network (`localhost`, `127.0.0.1`, `192.168.x.x`, `10.x.x.x`, `*.local`).
- **An SMTP account** for outgoing email — it carries your warnings and your recipients' release
    links.

## Quick start

<Steps>
    <Step title="Get the core">
        Clone [the repository](https://github.com/Nickbur/quietletter_core) and copy the production
        settings file `env/env.prod` to `.env`:

        ```bash
        git clone https://github.com/Nickbur/quietletter_core.git
        cd quietletter_core
        cp env/env.prod .env
        ```
    </Step>
    <Step title="Fill in your settings">
        Generate a strong token (`openssl rand -hex 32`) and set `OWNER_TOKEN` — it's how your app
        authenticates to your instance. Then replace the remaining `REPLACE_ME` values: your instance's
        public URL, your email for check-in warnings, and your SMTP details. The server won't start while
        any `REPLACE_ME` value is left; to keep mail in the console, leave `SMTP_HOST` empty.
    </Step>
    <Step title="Run it">
        `docker compose up --build -d` starts PostgreSQL and the server, applies the database migrations,
        and begins the check-in sweep. The server listens on port 8080 — point your HTTPS reverse proxy at
        it.
    </Step>
</Steps>

Configuration, email, backups, upgrades, running without Docker and the full API are covered in
[Configure, operate & API](/quietletter/running-your-instance).

## Connect the app

In the app, open **Settings → Self-hosted instance** (or tap **Connect your instance** on the sign-in
screen) and enter the **Instance URL** and your **Owner token**. The app checks that it can reach the
instance, signs out of the hosted account on that device, and reloads against your instance — no
account needed. The menu then shows your instance's address.

To go back, choose **Use the hosted service** in the same place and sign in again.

<Note>
    The web app at quietletter.burakov.net runs over HTTPS, so it can't reach an instance served over
    plain HTTP, even on your local network. Use HTTPS, or the iPhone / Android app.
</Note>

## How your instance differs from the hosted service

- **One owner, everything unlocked.** An instance serves one person — whoever holds the owner token.
    There are no accounts, plans or limits.
- **Email is the delivery channel.** Push and SMS warning steps are only written to the server's log
    (you can wire up your own provider). The app's own reminders before each deadline still work on
    your device.
- **Warning emails** say to open your Quietletter client to check in, rather than linking to the
    hosted web app.
- **Public pages.** An instance can publish a truly public page when a switch fires — a feature the
    hosted service doesn't offer. See [Configure, operate & API](/quietletter/running-your-instance).

## Move your switches

Your switches move between the hosted service and your own instance — in either direction — as a
single file. See [Back up and move your switches](/quietletter/backup-and-transfer).

## Continuity

- **Export anytime.** You can always export every switch to one file and import it into your own
    instance — or back — so your setup is never trapped inside one service.
- **If the hosted service ever winds down**, you get long advance notice, a final export, and a
    migration guide to self-hosting.
- **Honest limits.** "It outlives any company" is only partly solvable — self-hosting is the real
    backstop, and an instance is only as reliable as whoever runs it.
